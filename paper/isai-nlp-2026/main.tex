\title{Prompt Position, Not Prompt Content:\\
Three Ordering Failures in a Thai\\
Retrieval-Augmented Question Answering System}

\begin{abstract}
Evaluations of retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) routinely attribute answer
quality to the retriever. We report three cases from a production-intent Thai
RAG system in which the number being measured was set by where an instruction
sat in the prompt rather than by retrieval, and in which the prompt's content
was held fixed while only its ordering changed. On a corpus of 2{,}853 Thai
academic-council resolutions with 106 entity-anchored queries and 1{,}046
relevance judgments (9.87 relevant documents per query), we find: (i) the
conventional fix of placing instructions \emph{after} the retrieved context
interacts with the serving runtime's front-truncation policy to convert a loud
failure into a silent one, damaging 81 of 1{,}590 generated answers with no
observable symptom; (ii) a brevity instruction (``answer in at most three
sentences'') acts as a citation-recall cap, and replacing it raises citation
recall significantly on two of four answering arms (up to $+0.0871$,
Holm-adjusted $p=0.0000$, family size $m=9$) with no change to retrieval; and
(iii) a prohibition already present in the prompt is defeated by a later,
more recent rule --- moving it after the offending rule and declaring its
precedence takes closed-book hallucinations from 24 of 106 to 1 of 106 on
\texttt{gemma4:e4b}, and phantom citations from 37/37 to 1/1, while leaving the
four answering arms essentially unchanged. All three effects are invisible to
retrieval metrics. We give the detection signature for (i), report every
comparison with its Holm family size, and state the generator noise floor that
bounds what a single-cell difference can mean.
\end{abstract}

retrieval-augmented generation, prompt engineering, Thai NLP, information

\section{Introduction}

A retrieval-augmented question answering system has two halves that are usually
evaluated separately: a retriever, scored with ranking metrics against
relevance judgments, and a generator, scored on the answer it produces from the
retrieved context~\cite{lewis2020rag}. When end-to-end answer quality is
reported, the discussion almost always concerns the retriever --- which
embedder, which fusion, which reranker.

This paper reports three findings from building a Thai RAG system over
institutional meeting minutes, in each of which the \emph{prompt's layout}, not
its content and not the retriever, determined the measured outcome. The three
are not variations of one effect; they act at different layers, and each would
have been reported as a different kind of result had it not been isolated:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Layout versus runtime truncation.} Placing instructions after the
retrieved documents is the standard defence against an over-long prompt losing
its rules. On our serving runtime it makes truncation \emph{invisible instead of
harmless}: the rules always survive, the best-ranked evidence is deleted, and
the answer is well-formed, correctly shaped in its citations, and evidence-poor
for reasons nothing in the output reveals.
\item \textbf{A brevity instruction is a recall cap.} Against a judgment set
averaging 9.87 relevant documents per query, an instruction to answer in at most
three sentences cannot cite them. The resulting flat citation recall reads as a
generator ceiling and is a prompt budget.
\item \textbf{A rule's position beats its presence.} A rule forbidding answers
that the supplied documents do not support was identical, word for word, in both
prompt variants we compare. Under one variant a second model fabricated answers
on 24 of 106 zero-document queries. The repair was not a new prohibition but the
same prohibition moved after the rule that was defeating it, with an explicit
precedence declaration.
\end{enumerate}

Effect (1) is a systems interaction; effect (2) is a measurement artifact that
would silently understate any RAG system evaluated this way; effect (3) is a
safety failure with a one-line fix. What unites them is that in all three the
prompt's \emph{content} was adequate and its \emph{order} was not.

\textbf{Contributions.} (i) Three measured prompt-ordering effects in a
non-English, entity-anchored RAG setting, each isolated with the rest of the
system held fixed; (ii) an exact, cheap detection signature for silent prompt
truncation under a widely used local-inference runtime; (iii) a prompt-repair
rule --- \emph{declare precedence and move the rule after its competitor, rather
than adding a stronger prohibition} --- validated on two generators with a
built-in control; and (iv) an explicit account of the generator noise floor and
of multiple-comparison family sizes, so that the null cells reported here can be
read as bounds rather than as absence of effect.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Thai retrieval resources.} Thai is covered as a \emph{language} by
several multilingual retrieval benchmarks. MIRACL~\cite{zhang2023miracl}
includes Thai Wikipedia ad-hoc retrieval with natural queries written by native
speakers, at roughly 9--10 labels per query; Mr.~TyDi~\cite{zhang2021mrtydi}
includes Thai but is shallow, at about one positive passage per query.
BEIR~\cite{thakur2021beir} is English-only. Domain-specific Thai RAG evaluation
exists: NitiBench~\cite{nitibench2025} benchmarks LLM frameworks on Thai legal
question answering. What is not covered is the \emph{domain} we work in ---
administrative and academic-governance minutes --- and, more importantly for this
paper, none of these resources is designed to separate prompt effects from
retrieval effects, because none of them evaluates a generator at all.

\textbf{Position effects in language models.} That transformer language models
use long contexts unevenly is established: Liu et al.~\cite{liu2024lost} show a
U-shaped curve in which information at the beginning and end of a context is used
far more effectively than information in the middle. Our findings are adjacent
but distinct in two ways. First, the object whose position matters here is an
\emph{instruction}, not evidence: in finding (3) two rules compete and the later
one wins even though the earlier one is more specific and strictly prohibitive.
Second, finding (1) is not a model property at all --- it is an interaction
between prompt layout and a serving runtime's truncation policy, and it therefore
survives any improvement in the model's use of long context.

\textbf{Retrieval components.} Our arms use BM25~\cite{robertson2009bm25}, dense
retrieval with multilingual embedders including BGE-M3~\cite{chen2024bgem3}, and
reciprocal rank fusion~\cite{cormack2009rrf} for the hybrid arms. These are
background: no claim in this paper is about which of them is better.

\section{Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Corpus and query set}

The corpus is 2{,}853 Thai academic-council resolutions, obtained as scanned PDFs
and converted by OCR. Relevance is judged at the level of a \emph{resolution} ---
the unit a reader of these minutes actually wants --- rather than at the level of
a retrieval chunk.

The evaluation set contains 106 entity-anchored queries with 1{,}046 relevance
judgments, i.e. a mean of \textbf{9.87 relevant documents per query}. This depth
is the reason effect (2) is visible at all: against a shallow judgment set of
about one relevant document per query, an instruction capping the answer at three
sentences costs nothing measurable. Composition is given in
Table~\ref{tab:queries}.

\subsection{Arms}

Five arms supply the context. Four retrieve ten documents each: \texttt{hybrid}
(RRF over BM25 and a dense arm), \texttt{dense}, \texttt{bm25}, and a
deliberately weak \texttt{m2v} arm using a low-capacity static embedder. The
fifth, \texttt{closed\_book}, supplies \emph{no documents at all} and is the
control that carries finding (3): with an empty context, the only correct
behaviour is to abstain, and any answer produced is by construction ungrounded.

\subsection{Generators}

Both generators run locally through the same serving runtime (ollama) on a single
consumer GPU. \texttt{phi4} is the primary generator; \texttt{gemma4:e4b} is a
second-generator check, run precisely so that a prompt effect can be told apart
from a model idiosyncrasy. Sampling temperature is 0. Following a measurement
reported in Section~\ref{sec:limits}, temperature 0 is \emph{not} treated here as
reproducible.

\subsection{Metrics and statistics}

\emph{Citation recall} is the fraction of a query's gold resolutions that the
answer's citation line names, macro-averaged over all 106 queries.
\emph{Citation precision} is the fraction of cited documents that are gold,
macro-averaged over queries that cited at least one document. A \emph{phantom}
citation is a label that does not appear in the supplied context at all, reported
as a micro count over all citations. For finding (3) we also report the
abstention $2\times2$: whether the context contained gold, against whether the
model abstained; the cell with no gold and no abstention is a hallucination.

Significance is paired bootstrap over queries ($10{,}000$ resamples, fixed seed)
with Holm correction~\cite{holm1979}. \textbf{We report the Holm family size $m$
with every adjusted $p$-value}, because in this study the identical test on
identical data reads significant in one family and not in another purely through
$m$; a bare adjusted $p$ is ambiguous.

\section{Finding 1: Layout Interacts With Runtime Truncation}

\subsection{The loud failure, and the standard fix}

An initial pilot placed the instructions \emph{before} the retrieved documents.
On prompts that carried context it produced \textbf{0 of 4} correctly formatted
citations, while producing \textbf{4 of 4} on the short zero-document prompts ---
same model, same instructions, so not a capability limit. The cause is that the
runtime truncates an over-long prompt from the \emph{front}: with instructions
first, the rules were cut away before the model ever saw them. The standard fix
applies --- put the documents first and the rules last, so a truncation cannot
delete the rules.

\subsection{What the fix actually did}

The fix worked, and it converted a loud failure into a silent one. Because the
prompt lays the retrieved documents out best-ranked first and the rules last, an
over-long prompt now loses \emph{the highest-ranked evidence} and keeps every
rule. The result is an answer that is well-formed, cites in the correct format,
and abstains when told to --- and that is evidence-poor for reasons nothing in the
output reveals. Nothing warned; the runtime reports no error.

\subsection{The truncation rule, measured}

We measured the runtime's behaviour rather than reading it from documentation.
One 14{,}721-token prompt at three context sizes gives Table~\ref{tab:trunc}.

\begin{table}[t]
\caption{Prompt truncation, one 14{,}721-token prompt}
\label{tab:trunc}
\centering
\begin{tabular}{rrl}
\toprule
\texttt{num\_ctx} & \texttt{prompt\_eval\_count} & interpretation \\
\midrule
4{,}096  & 2{,}050  & cut to $\texttt{num\_ctx}/2 + 2$ \\
8{,}192  & 4{,}098  & cut to $\texttt{num\_ctx}/2 + 2$ \\
16{,}384 & 14{,}721 & fed whole \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

An intuitive hypothesis --- \emph{the runtime never feeds more than half the
context window} --- is refuted by its own control: prompts of 5{,}651, 6{,}885 and
7{,}508 tokens are fed \emph{whole} at $\texttt{num\_ctx}=8{,}192$. The rule that
reproduces all six observations is:

\begin{quote}
A prompt that fits \texttt{num\_ctx} is fed whole; a prompt that exceeds it is
cut to $\texttt{num\_ctx}/2 + 2$ tokens, keeping the \emph{tail}.
\end{quote}

The practical value of the second clause is that
$\texttt{prompt\_eval\_count} = \texttt{num\_ctx}/2 + 2$ is an \textbf{exact
detection signature}. It costs nothing: the runtime already returns
\texttt{prompt\_eval\_count} with every response. We therefore recommend
recording it per generated answer and asserting the signature, in preference to
any layout convention --- a layout rule chooses \emph{which half} of the prompt
dies, and cannot tell you \emph{that} something died.

\subsection{Blast radius}

Applying the signature retrospectively identified \textbf{81 of 1{,}590}
generated answers as produced from a truncated prompt, with as little as 31\% of
the intended prompt actually fed. Regenerating all 81 at
$\texttt{num\_ctx}=16{,}384$ flipped \textbf{1 of 57} verdicts in the primary
report and \textbf{3 of 57} in a secondary one; the prompt-ablation headline of
Section~\ref{sec:ablation} did not move. We stress the direction of that result:
the damage was real and the published conclusions largely survived it, which is
exactly the case in which a silent fault is never found by inspecting outcomes.

\section{Finding 2: A Brevity Instruction Caps Citation Recall}
\label{sec:ablation}

\subsection{Design}

The prompt is a numbered rule list. We hold rules 1--3 and the answer format
fixed and change only rule 4, giving the variants in Table~\ref{tab:variants}.
Rules 1--3 state, in Thai: (1) answer only from the supplied documents, never
from other knowledge; (2) cite the documents used by bracketed number; (3) if the
supplied documents do not contain the answer, reply \textsc{not-found} only, and
do not guess.

Citation recall rises significantly on \texttt{hybrid} ($+0.0871$) and
\texttt{bm25} ($+0.0789$), both at Holm-adjusted $p=0.0000$ with $m=9$.
\textbf{No retrieval changed}: the contexts are byte-identical between variants
and only rule 4 differs. The pre-existing reading of the flat baseline recall ---
that the generator could not do better --- is therefore wrong. It was a budget
imposed by the prompt.

\subsection{Two qualifications we do not want cited away}

First, the \texttt{dense} arm's gain is \textbf{not significant} ($+0.0407$, Holm
$p=0.6610$). We report it as a bound, not as an effect: the data rule out a loss
and do not establish a gain. The mechanism we suspect --- that a stronger context
reduces the marginal value of the instruction --- is consistent with
\texttt{dense} and \texttt{hybrid} being the two strongest arms, but we did not
test it and do not claim it.

Second, the gain is a \emph{trade}. Citation precision falls on three of four
arms, most on \texttt{bm25} ($-0.0644$), and while no precision cell is
significant at $m=9$, the direction is consistent. Instructing a model to cite
everything relevant produces more citations, and some of the extra ones are
wrong.

\subsection{The effect is not model-specific, but the levels are}

Under \texttt{cite\_all}, \texttt{gemma4:e4b} reaches citation recall 0.4956
(hybrid) and 0.5049 (dense), against \texttt{phi4}'s 0.3823 and 0.3145. The
direction of the prompt effect transfers between generators; \textbf{the levels
do not transfer at all}. Any absolute citation-recall figure reported for a RAG
system is a joint property of the retriever, the prompt and the generator, and is
not portable across a change in any of the three.

\section{Finding 3: A Rule Already Present, Defeated by Position}

\subsection{The failure}

Rule 3 --- \emph{if the supplied documents do not contain the answer, reply
\textsc{not-found} only, and do not guess} --- is present, identical and word for
word, in all three variants. It is a strict prohibition and it is stated before
any competing instruction.

It does not hold. Table~\ref{tab:closed} gives the \texttt{closed\_book} arm,
where the context is empty and abstention is the only correct output. Under
\texttt{cite\_all}, \texttt{gemma4:e4b} answers anyway on \textbf{24 of 106}
queries, and \emph{every one} of the 37 citations it produces is a phantom --- a
bracketed document number when no document was supplied at all. \texttt{phi4}
shows the same failure at much lower magnitude: 2 of 106, 5/5 phantom.

\subsection{The diagnosis: recency, not absence}

The natural reading is that the model ignores instructions. The comparison
refutes it. Rule 3 is \emph{identical} between \texttt{sentence\_cap} and
\texttt{cite\_all}, and under \texttt{sentence\_cap} \texttt{phi4} hallucinates
0 times. What changed is the rule that follows it: \texttt{cite\_all}'s rule 4
instructs the model to cite every relevant document, it is the last line before
the question, and \emph{with nothing to cite it reads as an instruction to
produce citations}. The specific, prohibitive, earlier rule loses to the general,
permissive, later one.

\subsection{The repair, and why its shape matters}

The repair is not a stronger prohibition. It is the same prohibition, moved:
rule 5 restates the zero-document case \emph{after} rule 4 and declares
precedence in as many words --- \emph{this rule outranks rule 4}. Rule 6 adds a
ceiling on which labels are citable, aimed at the bounded-context version of the
same over-production (the \texttt{dense} arm produced 4 phantom labels against 5
supplied documents).

The effect is in Table~\ref{tab:closed}: \textbf{24 hallucinations become 1}, and
37/37 phantom citations become 1/1. \texttt{phi4} goes from 2 to 0.

\subsection{The control that makes this readable}

A guard that suppressed hallucination by making the model reticent everywhere
would be worthless. The four answering arms are the built-in control, and they
barely move: for \texttt{gemma4:e4b} the abstention $2\times2$ is \emph{identical}
between \texttt{cite\_all} and \texttt{cite\_all\_guarded} on \texttt{hybrid}
(93/9/2/2) and on \texttt{dense} (92/8/4/2), and moves by at most three cells on
\texttt{bm25} and \texttt{m2v}. Citation recall on the strong arms is unchanged or
slightly higher under the guard (hybrid $0.4956 \rightarrow 0.5260$). The guard
reached the case it was aimed at and left the rest alone.

\subsection{Where the failure concentrates}

In \emph{both} models, 100\% of closed-book hallucination occurs on
\texttt{course} queries. We report this as an observation, not a mechanism: a
course code is a short, highly patterned string about which a language model has
strong priors, and the plausible explanation --- that a well-formed guess is
easier to produce for this query shape --- was not tested here.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limits}

\textbf{Temperature 0 is not reproducible, and we measured how badly.} Feeding
byte-identical prompts to \texttt{phi4} at temperature 0 reproduces the identical
citation set only \textbf{14 of 24} times under \texttt{cite\_all}. Every claim in
this paper is therefore made over families of 106 queries with Holm-corrected
paired bootstrap, and no single-cell difference is interpreted. A reader
reproducing this work should expect cell-level disagreement, and should
re-measure this noise floor rather than infer determinism from the temperature
setting.

\textbf{Holm family size is load-bearing.} In our data the identical test on
identical data reads significant in a family of 9 and not significant in a family
of 24. We quote $m$ everywhere; a comparison from Table~\ref{tab:family2} should
not be quoted against a $p$-value computed in a different family.

\textbf{A generation cap can masquerade as a retrieval result.} The maximum
generated length is capped at 4{,}096 tokens. \textbf{3 of 1{,}272} \texttt{phi4}
cells reach that cap, all of them \texttt{course} queries. A capped answer never
reaches its citation line and is therefore scored as having cited nothing --- a
generator failure that reads as a retrieval failure. The count is small enough not
to move any conclusion here, and is stated because the failure mode is invisible
in the metric.

\textbf{The judgments are rule-derived.} Relevance judgments were derived by
string containment of each query's anchor entity, which under-counts documents
phrasing that entity differently. Citation recall inherits the undercount. It is a
level effect shared by every arm and variant, and the comparisons here are paired,
so it does not threaten the contrasts --- but absolute values should be read as a
lower bound.

\textbf{Scope.} One corpus, one language, one serving runtime, two generators.
Finding (1) is specific to a runtime that truncates from the front and should be
re-measured, not assumed, elsewhere; the \emph{method} --- assert a signature on
the runtime's own reported token count --- transfers. Findings (2) and (3) were
each observed on two generators, with the direction preserved and the levels not.

\section{Conclusion}

Three times in one system, the quantity we were measuring was set by where an
instruction sat rather than by what it said. Placing rules after the context is
the correct fix for one failure and silently creates another, and the durable
defence is a truncation signature rather than a layout convention. A brevity
instruction, harmless against a shallow judgment set, acts as a citation-recall
cap against a deep one --- and the ceiling it produces is easily mistaken for a
generator limit. And a prohibition already written into the prompt is defeated by
a later rule, with a repair that consists of moving it and declaring precedence
rather than strengthening it.

For practitioners the operational form is short: record the runtime's reported
prompt token count and assert it; check whether an instruction constrains the
quantity you are about to report; and when a rule is disobeyed, look at what
follows it before rewriting it. For evaluation the point is sharper --- a RAG
result that does not hold the prompt fixed, and say so, may be reporting a
property of its instructions.
